Art as the Throughline

Adam Himoff’s story has taken many turns, but the road always leads back to art

Summer/Fall 2026

Written By: Tiffini Porter | Images: Deborah DeKoff

When Adam Himoff was 13, his mother enrolled him in a figure drawing class at the Art Students League of New York. Founded in 1875, the League has been a legendary training ground for artists like Jackson Pollock, Norman Rockwell, and Georgia O’Keeffe.

Placing a young teen in an adult class, complete with live nude models, was a bold move. “I thought that was hilarious,” Adam recalls, “but there’s no room to giggle when you’re the only kid there. So, I just got to work, and it was incredible.”

Reminiscing at his home studio in Park City’s Pinebrook neighborhood, he counts himself lucky to have grown up in Manhattan with a parent who
encouraged his artistic spirit. Exploring New York’s endless options—from masterworks at the Met to graffiti on city streets—set a foundation for a
life shaped by creativity. “Art is not a luxury,” he says, reflecting on the value it has brought to him and his family. “It’s a necessity that elevates communities.”

Describing himself as both a “sensitive art kid” and a quintessential competitive New Yorker, the duality in Adam’s personality led him to follow many paths. He started out as an economics major before earning a degree in English literature at Dartmouth and later pursuing an
MBA at UCLA. His career has taken him across the country, spanning finance, venture capital, film production, and more. Through it all, artmaking was a quiet constant if not his primary pursuit.

In 2013, Adam moved from Los Angeles to Park City with his wife, Andrea, and two young daughters. Andrea had grown up in Aspen, and their aim was to offer their children a similar mountain-town upbringing. Adam spent the next decade building an industrial recruiting business in Salt Lake City. While working from home during the pandemic, he produced an artwork that would change the trajectory of his life.

Entitled Frederick Douglass / Liberty, the work reimagines the 19th-century abolitionist in
contemporary context. Originally created as a linocut print, it highlights Adam’s signature “line style,” utilizing parallel and cross-hatched mark-making to form images with a sense of depth and high energy.

The print quickly drew attention on social media and was acquired by the American Civil Liberties Union. Momentum grew from there, and it was later adapted for mural installations in Easton and Baltimore, Maryland. The piece drew excitement, as well as some controversy, and Adam was suddenly in the spotlight.

The Power of Art

In late 2022, a year before the first mural debuted, Adam finalized the sale of his recruiting company and turned his focus to making art full time. Within hours, he boarded a plane to attend his first art fair in Miami, where he met Aldy Milliken, executive director of Kimball Art Center. The two made a connection and, in hindsight, everything began to come together. After a lifetime of making art but not quite identifying as an artist, Adam now has a thriving contemporary practice and serves as board chair for Park City’s flagship art venue as it embarks on an ambitious plan to build a permanent facility in Kimball Junction.

“Adam possesses the ideal blend of experiences for the Kimball Art Center at the moment: business experience that includes organizational development aligned perfectly for our current stage, combined with someone who has rekindled a passion for art and the artistic process,” Aldy says. “He soaks up our programs, activities, and educational mission, which allows him to speak authentically about our mission and vision.”

Adam’s work with Kimball Art Center is tied to his own journey and belief in the power of art. When asked about plans for the Center’s future, he points to founder Bill Kimball as a continuing source of inspiration. “We were born out of a garage,” Adam says, invoking Kimball’s grassroots, open-door approach and belief in making art accessible to everyone. After 50 years of adaptation and growth, he sees the organization as more engaged in national and global dialogues, while still dedicated to serving the community.

As Park City prepares for the 2034 Olympics and another turn on the international stage, Adam looks forward to helping create a local space where people can simultaneously explore their connection to art and nature. For him, art is no longer a parallel track, but the central path guiding his way.
