Canada ramps up ability to fight wars as peacekeeping role recedes

Peacekeeping operations once romanticized as the backbone of Canada’s international nobility have all but vanished amid preparations for war.

By Alex BallingallDeputy Ottawa Bureau Chief

OTTAWA — Back in February this year, over 11 days at a military base in Kingston, Ont., the Canadian Army was at war. 

At least, it pretended it was. 

The “division-level war game” that played out that month simulated — on computer screens, whiteboards, and tabletop battle maps — what it would be like to fight a “high-intensity conflict against a peer adversary,” the military explained in a subsequent statement.

The game tested several aspects of modern combat, from how electronic warfare and intelligence can support the fighting, to how long-range projectiles affect the enemy before a direct clash of ground forces.

Not so long ago, such exercises were the realm of hobbyists and not a big part of serious military planning. But now “war gaming is the sexiest thing on the planet,” said Jean-François Bélanger, an assistant professor at the Royal Danish Defence College in Copenhagen. 

The reason for its spike in popularity is simple, he said: The threats, the risks, the chessboard of international relations — all of it has changed. “Now we need to think in terms of war-fighting,” Bélanger said. 

Bolstered by the Carney government’s massive boost to defence spending, and against the backdrop of a world that analysts see as riven with danger, great-power competition and the morphing stance of the United States under its mercurial president, the Canadian military is steadily marching in this direction. Peacekeeping operations once romanticized as the backbone of Canada’s international nobility have all but vanished. The notion of small expeditionary contributions abroad is fading, too. And what is emerging is a mindset that the country’s soldiers need to be ready for what is undoubtedly the worst-case scenario: major war. 

But while many see these preparations as unavoidable, some also worry over a risk inherent to this shift. As Canada and other countries beef up militarily for a new reality, one that former chief of the defence staff Wayne Eyre has said will require “hard power” to prepare for — and hopefully prevent — the next major global conflict, there is a chance that those preparations actually make the next great-power confrontation more likely to happen, or to break out sooner. 

“Personally, I’m really concerned,” said Philippe Beaulieu-Brossard, who teaches at the Canadian Forces College in Toronto.

“This may become a self-fulfilling prophecy.” 

According to Beaulieu-Brossard, some inside the Canadian Armed Forces refer to this nightmare possibility as “Jupiter” — World War III, basically. There are signs the military is thinking about it, or something like it. In a report published last year, the Canadian Army concluded that it “must be prepared to conduct Major Combat Operations” as a “core component of its strategic posture.”

The report defined these as “large-scale, high-intensity” operations involving “sustained combat against peer or near-peer adversaries,” which means “capable” state opponents, armed forces spokesperson Capt. David Feil said by email. 

The report adds that the army as it stands was built for the “contribution warfare” of recent decades, in which Canadian soldiers took part in missions of multinational coalitions abroad, but must now be “structured, trained, and equipped” for larger conflicts. The goal is to be ready within the next five to 15 years for such a scenario, underscoring the “need to modernize and prepare now,” Feil said. 

Last month, the Department of National Defence also quietly announced it is “rebalancing” remaining personnel devoted to peacekeeping missions in South Sudan, the Democratic Republic of Congo, Kosovo, Cyprus and the Sinai Peninsula. The change prompted some to claim Canada is now turning its back on a proud tradition that stretches back to Lester B. Pearson’s Nobel Peace Prize-winning contribution to the first major UN peacekeeping mission of the Suez Crisis in 1956. 

“Peacekeeping should not be sacrificed at the altar of war-fighting,” said Walter Dorn, a professor of defence studies at the Royal Military College of Canada in Kingston. He noted that Canada’s contribution to UN peacekeeping missions had already dropped from more than 3,000 personnel in the 1990s to between 20 and 30 in recent years.

With almost $82 billion in new defence funding earmarked over the next five years, and tens of billions more promised on top of that by 2035, Dorn argued the military should have the capacity to both prepare for the worst and contribute more meaningfully to UN peacekeeping. 

“Venus and Mars can both exist without having Mars obliterate Venus,” he said. 

In an interview with the Star, Defence Minister David McGuinty said Canada’s military is still engaged in many ways, and cited the use of transport planes devoted to Canada’s Russian deterrence operation in Latvia in helping deliver materiel for UN operations in Mali and Ghana and other African countries.

But McGuinty recognized that Canada’s “posture has changed,” and that it is doing so out of necessity. Since launching a full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, Russia has transformed into a “war economy,” he said. Iran, still locked in conflict with the U.S., has shown the capability of launching missiles that could reach European cities, he added. And there are concerns about Russian and Chinese activity in the Arctic, where the government is keen to shore up a military presence and assert Canada’s sovereignty over a vast and formidable area. 

“There’s no doubt the threat landscape continues to evolve,” he said. “We have to be fleet-footed. We have to be adaptable.”

McGuinty also said he doesn’t think of Canada’s military rebuild as preparation for a major conflict. Instead, he said it is a long-overdue correction to years of underspending to take more responsibility for the country’s own protection. That includes everything from raising soldiers’ salaries to funding more military housing, expanding domestic bases, bolstering surveillance of the North and acquiring new and expensive hardware, like a fleet of modern submarines and next-generation fighter jets. 

“These are investments to protect the peace,” McGuinty said. “Hopefully, as we do this, we’re going to continue to maintain that peace.” 

For Alex Wilner, a Carleton University professor who studies military deterrence theory, McGuinty is articulating the notion that a stronger military can avert conflict by intimidating would-be adversaries. It’s consistent with an emerging view of a more unstable world, he explained — one in which China is eyeing Taiwan for a possible military takeover, Russia is at war in Ukraine and threatening NATO’s eastern and northern flanks, and the U.S. under President Donald Trump is goading allies like Canada to boost their own militaries and raising questions about its commitment to collective defence. 

“If the new normal is anything like what it’s been in the last couple of years, then we have to get ready,” Wilner said.

The risk in getting ready is that what is meant as a defensive signal is interpreted as offensive, in which adversaries develop increasingly aggressive postures until a conflict is triggered, Wilner explained. Getting deterrence right as the world grows more dangerous will be more important, especially if Canada has to assert itself alone or in new ways in some domains like the Arctic and in cyberspace, Wilner added. 

Beaulieu-Brossard said he worries that rearmament and assumptions about warfare could indeed bring the next conflict close. Russia’s invasion of Ukraine in 2022 could have been prompted in part by a decision to launch the war before Western countries and their support for Kyiv got stronger, he said. China’s ambitions for control of Taiwan could take a similar route, with the U.S. currently bogged down in its conflict with Iran, he added. 

“That’s where I think governments need to be extremely responsible in the way they communicate all of these rearmament efforts,” Beaulieu-Brossard said. 

“It’s very unsettling, but sadly, it’s actually the task of militaries around this world to think of the worst-case scenario.” 

That reality makes peacekeeping efforts all the more vital, Dorn argued. The First World War began because a nationalist assassin murdered the heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne in Sarajevo, while the Second World War erupted after Hitler’s Germany asserted its claims to territory in neighbouring countries.

“The lessons of the two World Wars are that small things matter,” he said. 

The hope is that there isn’t a third one to learn from next.
